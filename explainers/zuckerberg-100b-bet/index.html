00 · OVERVIEWThe 12 things that matter

The episode in brief. Each point links to the sections and quotes below.

  1. Muse Wonder launches as a frontier model in two versions, Wonder and Wonder Full. It is a new and much bigger pre-train (codename Watermelon) than the Muse Spark line (Avocado), and Meta calls it state of the art for personal and professional agents.
  2. Memory is the main bet. “Active memory” is trained into the model, and the model does its own compaction. They contrast this directly with OpenClaw’s harness-level compaction, which they call “buggy”.
  3. Four headline capabilities: active memory, active focus (persisting on long tasks), domain expertise (finance and legal evals), and frontier coding (DeepSWE, SWE-Marathon, Terminal-Bench 4).
  4. “The model is the product.” Personality, proactivity, discretion and reliability are set through post-training, not the UI. There is deliberately no model picker and no effort picker.
  5. Personal superintelligence, not knowledge-work automation. They argue the other labs are fixated on coding and automating knowledge work, and that the “highest use” of AI is invention and creation.
  6. Alignment means alignment with the user. The agent should do what you ask, with no “moralizing lecture”, refuse gracefully only when something is catastrophic, and practise discretion: doing things for you while revealing as little as possible about you.
  7. A frontier lab means betting everything up front. Tens of billions per model, data centres planned years ahead. They call it “the opposite of Lean Startup”. The CFO warned in Hawaii that if the model failed, things would be “really, really, really bad.”
  8. The downside is backstopped. If Meta’s models fail, other companies still want the compute. For the bet to be a total loss, “AI overall has to fail.”
  9. Agentic commerce has started. People are handing purchases to agents. Agents will hold their own accounts, which grows the total market. Every business will run agents, and browsing websites is a temporary bridge.
  10. After the phone: glasses and gadgets. Fully multimodal glasses, possibly with no app store. Muse Gadgets (such as HomeLink) and phone makers building Muse in. Voice input is taking off, but voice is a poor way to receive information.
  11. It won’t be winner-take-all. Lead labs make execution mistakes, distillation helps followers catch up, and talent picks sides. A diverse ecosystem is also safer.
  12. How MSL was built: a full reboot after Llama 4, a team chosen for talent density (fewer than 200 researchers), a flat structure with no non-technical managers, no deadlines, and a CEO who went “AI-pilled” and moved the whole company behind it.

01 · LAUNCHMeet Muse Wonder

The first MSL model that Meta says sits at the frontier, rather than just catching up to it.

Line-up

Muse Spark → Muse Wonder → Wonder Full

  • Spark: based on the pre-train from Dec 2025. Versions 1 → 1.3 only added more RL on the same base model.
  • Wonder: a new pre-train that is “significantly bigger” and serves as Meta’s frontier model.
  • Wonder Full: a “much higher-end” version, offered to Muse Code subscribers. (The name prompted a dad joke: “Wonder full.”)
Availability

Where you can use it

  • Muse app: paid subscriptions first, because Wonder is expensive to serve. Free users stay on Spark for now.
  • Meta Model API and OpenRouter.
  • Muse Code users; Wonder Full for Muse Code subscribers.
  • The goal: Wonder-level quality “in the Spark form factor” for hundreds of millions of people.
Code names

Avocado → Watermelon → ?

Spark was Avocado and Wonder was Watermelon. “We’re out of fruits”: the only bigger fruit is the durian, which nobody wants. A Muse-ordered watermelon later shows up on the livestream and in the episode’s live demo.

The four headline capabilities

01
Active memory

Remembers what you tell it across days, weeks and months, and uses it in later tasks. The model is “aware of its memory as a core system” and handles its own compaction. Measured with Meta’s internal Memory Bench.

02
Active focus

Persistence: finishing complete tasks “over very long horizons”, and when it hits setbacks, finding other solutions “in an aligned way”. Early testers say this is what stands out most.

03
Domain expertise

“A team of experts in your corner”: legal, finance, medical and tax. Meta says it is state of the art on many finance and legal evals. This expertise comes from pre-training data, not just RL.

04
Frontier coding

State of the art on DeepSWE and SWE-Marathon, strong on Terminal-Bench 4. Coders are a “minority” of Muse users, but they will see a big jump.

On how it compares with rivals: John asks whether Astra, Fable and Wonder are “altogether at the frontier”. Mark answers “you can look at it empirically by the benchmarks”, and Alex claims it is “the best model in the world for the use case of personal professional agents.”

02 · DEEP DIVEMemory: the core of the pitch

The biggest technical difference they claim. Many people’s first experience of AI agents has been unreliable memory, and Meta says it fixed this in the model itself rather than in the harness around it.

What “memory” means here

Asked how memory differs from context, Alex separates the two. Context is the working memory for the task in front of the model. Memory is about the experience over time: does the model remember what you told it weeks ago, and use it on a new task without being reminded?

Mark adds that compaction (summarizing older context so it fits) is “things that the model does”. John sums it up as “It’s not a harness, it’s in the model itself.” Alex confirms.

Why it matters for agents

  • Alex calls memory “one of the clear requirements to build reliable agents.”
  • Meta says it invested in memory “disproportionately” compared with other labs.
  • Memory is also what makes the agent feel like a character. Alex says the Muse “little guy” only works because it has persistent memory, can take actions, and has a personality.
  • It powers personalization: one view of you across family, friends and colleagues, instead of separate recommendation systems that don’t talk to each other.
  • They admit it isn’t solved: “still plenty of room to improve.”

04 · PRODUCT THESIS“The model is the product”

John argues that winning AI products quickly converge on one simple shape (transformers, then chat, now persistent agents like OpenClaw and Muse). Mark and Alex disagree: the important differences are real, but they live inside the model.

Why the products look the same but aren’t

  • Alex compares it to early mobile: “it’s just an app”, until it turned out there was “a lot of nuance in what exactly an app is.”
  • What sets agents apart is how proactive they are, when they step in, and how it feels when they talk to you. All of this is written down as model behaviour specs, training data and evals.
  • Nat Friedman’s product team mainly improves the product by post-training the model, not by changing the UI.
  • The “complex product work” outside the model goes into privacy and security.

Breakout use cases mentioned

  • Alex: his Muse, “Oiler”, sources “increasingly obscure teas” from farms in Taiwan and Japan every week.
  • Mark: an MMA coach. Cameras in his gym feed video to Muse, which gives feedback in the 60-second breaks between rounds. Mark kept telling it to “optimize yourself”. He also uses it as his personal eval.
  • Mark: weekend baking with his 3-year-old daughter, planned in voice mode, with Instacart orders that arrive on time. It added a watermelon to reach the delivery minimum.
  • Mark: HomeLink dims the lights across the house when it’s time to wind down.

05 · HOW IT’S MADEHow frontier models are actually built

A rare walkthrough of the pipeline from both the CEO and the lab head.

STAGE 1 · PRE-TRAIN
Huge data, huge compute

The public internet, books and proprietary data, plus images, audio and video for perception. Domain data (health, legal, finance) goes in early: “you don’t just want to RL that in at the end.” Data can transfer positively or negatively. Few samples, high stakes: “7 stages of grief.” Training climbs a ladder of models about 4–5× bigger at each step.

STAGE 2 · SFT
Seed the behaviours

Supervised fine-tuning gives the model the question and the ideal answer. This is where personality is shaped. By the end, a behaviour is “baked in” but appears only about 10% of the time.

STAGE 3 · RL
Draw the behaviours out

Reinforcement learning raises how often the right behaviour shows up. Building environments and tasks is the craft. “Less is actually more” for RL data. Bad environments can teach the model that misaligned behaviour is the only way to succeed, so they need curating.

STAGE 4 · SCALE ALL
Grow everything together

Grow pre-training, the RL recipe, RL compute and the agent harness at the same time. Spark 1 → 1.3 “feels night and day” from RL alone. Every lab is getting much more out of the same base models.

Team & time

  • Each stage has tens of people at Meta, compared with “hundreds” elsewhere. The research team is under 200, “closer to 100”, plus infrastructure staff.
  • Lots of compute per researcher, not spread thin over competing efforts.
  • Wonder was planned from August of the previous year, and Spark was a “stepping stone”.
  • Early signals: perplexity evals during pre-training, benchmarks during RL. Researchers held “analog protractors” to their monitors to read the slope of loss curves.
  • When they knew Wonder was good: after pre-training, the team warned that RL might take a while to beat Spark. It beat Spark almost immediately.

Compute economics

  • A lab’s strength is “how big its largest single computer is”.
  • Prometheus (Ohio) is over 1 GW. Hyperion (Louisiana) will reach 5 GW over the next few years. Both were planned in 2023–24, before MSL existed.
  • About $15B to run 1 GW for a year; about $50–60B in capex to build it.
  • Research ablations cost roughly as much as the main training run. Then there is serving.
  • A frontier model costs “tens of billions of dollars”.

06 · PHILOSOPHYAlignment, discretion & evals

Alignment = serving the user

Mark says alignment is “a very philosophical concept”, and the real question is aligned to whom. Meta’s answer is the person the agent works for. In practice that means doing what was asked, reliably and without lecturing, and declining gracefully when a request is catastrophic. Alex adds that alignment is “holistic” and spans the whole RL process, not a separate safety step.

Discretion: a new capability

Mark’s example: if you’re pregnant or have an allergy, your agent should book a suitable restaurant without disclosing your medical condition. Coding agents work where everyone shares the same context, so they never needed this skill.

Against benchmaxing

Public benchmarks are useful for finding “signs of life” while a lab is ramping up, but with each release Meta relies more on internal evals. Its “big model smell” metric measures the gap between in-distribution and out-of-distribution performance, and Alex says it improved sharply from Spark to Wonder.

The capability spreadsheet

Product quality comes from a carefully maintained spreadsheet of small capabilities. Each matters about 2% to users, but together they make models “that feel magical”.

07 · THE BETBetting $100B before seeing the cards

John’s framing: “you have to push your chips all in before you’ve been dealt the cards.”

The Hawaii moment

At an exec offsite at the end of last year, Mark was about to commit $100–200B with only Alex’s “vibe report” and a few plots as evidence. CFO Susan Li took Alex aside several times to stress that the model had to work. Some executives pushed to be “a little more conservative”. Mark’s answer was “we’re going to burn the boats.”

Why Mark thinks it’s less risky than it looks

  1. If AI works at all, “plenty of other people want all our compute”, and would pay more than $15B per GW.
  2. Even if AI as a whole fails, Meta has other productive uses for the compute.
  3. A slow launch would still have been fine. Mark’s median expectation was a few iterations to product-market fit. Instead Muse was a home run, and growth tooling wasn’t even ready.

08 · ECONOMYAgentic commerce

Phase 1

Browser use as a bridge

Strong browser and computer use are trained in, so businesses don’t need to integrate with Muse. John calls this the “ultimate backwards compatibility layer.”

Phase 2

Direct APIs

Key partners get direct API access, which is faster and more efficient. Mark says many more businesses are asking to integrate than are trying to block agents.

Phase 3

Every business has agents

Muse agents talk to business agents, especially Meta’s own. Agents get their own accounts, so the total market grows.

Value to users

  • Less friction in research and checkout.
  • Built-in constraints, e.g. “I need something by Wednesday”. John: relying on keyword search “was insane”.
  • Personalization from a complete picture of your life, e.g. a gift that suits your friend group.

Signals from the market

  • Mark signed up to Instacart for his Muse and now orders once or twice a week, where before it was about twice a year. That is new demand, not just a better conversion rate.
  • Stripe’s agentic commerce stats “only as of September basically started going vertical” (after announcements at Sessions in May).
  • Alex says he told Patrick Collison in 2023 that Stripe was “behind the 8-ball” on agentic commerce: “directionally correct, but … 3 years early.”

09 · FORM FACTORSBeyond the smartphone

Why voice assistants struggled

  • Conversation timing: pauses, interruptions, talking over each other.
  • Older assistants couldn’t do anything, so talking to them felt pointless. Once an agent can act, it feels “much closer to … talking to a human”.
  • Voice is high-bandwidth for input but slow for output. The ideal interface is “not just strictly voice-voice”.
  • Voice input is booming: researchers dictate into their keyboards, and college students “word vomit” for 10–15 minutes and let the AI organize it.

The hardware roadmap discussed

  • Glasses: camera and audio alone are useful. Small-display glasses are already “pretty powerful”. Eventually fully multimodal (voice in and out, sees what you see, “paint the pixels”), possibly with no app store.
  • Muse Gadgets: an ecosystem for new device types. Phone makers are already integrating it.
  • HomeLink: a nickel-sized USB device that connects Muse to everything in your home (Sonos, lights).
  • The live demo: John asks Muse by voice to order a watermelon to Stripe’s office.

10 · BUSINESS MODELHow do you fund the AI buildout?

Muse revenue

  • Free at scale, because network effects and partner integrations depend on reach.
  • Subscriptions for expensive features (Wonder).
  • A small cut of the money Muse makes or saves you.
  • A “business in a box” for small businesses.

Platform revenue

  • The API: the models are general, so they create a “large surplus of value”.
  • The new Meta Enterprise Platform, with CJ Desai joining to build it, selling Muse Wonder to enterprises.
  • OpenRouter distribution.

Structural advantages

  • A core ads business worth hundreds of billions that is still growing fast. Using the models to grow it a few percent faster pays for much of the effort.
  • Owning data centres means capex can be depreciated. Researcher salaries can’t be capitalized, and cloud renters pay ongoing rates.
  • GPUs keep their value: “even like an H100” is worth more than a year ago.
  • Meta’s playbook: reach a billion people, then scale revenue.

11 · MARKET STRUCTUREWhy AI won’t be winner-take-all

The 2023 story was that one lab gets ahead, starts recursive self-improvement, and pulls away. Alex gives three reasons it hasn’t happened.

Reason 1

Execution averages out

Every lab goes through strong and weak stretches. A lead holds only if the leader executes perfectly.

Reason 2

Distillation

“Nobody planned” for distillation to work this well. It puts strong capability into small models, which benefits open source and serving at scale.

Reason 3

People choose

Researchers, customers and dealmakers deliberately push toward a more balanced outcome. Many joined MSL to give AI to billions of people.

Mark’s safety argument for diversity

Many models from many companies, widely available, act as checks and balances. A few strong models held by a couple of companies is “a riskier configuration”. Mark also praises recent NVIDIA moves supporting open source, and OpenRouter as infrastructure for a diverse ecosystem. Alex adds that benchmark leaderboards hide real differences in “texture” between models, and that developers need a “wine sommelier” level of understanding.

The operating principles

  • Talent density over headcount, with lots of compute per researcher.
  • A full reboot, decided while recruiting, because top people wanted to start fresh. Strong people from Llama and FAIR were still brought in.
  • A flat structure (close to 200 direct reports in theory). The point is the “absence of non-technical managers.”
  • Stay technical: use the agents and write code. Managers who stop doing hands-on work fall behind quickly.
  • No deadlines, relying on “insecure overachievers” who push themselves. Alex records their pessimistic forecasts and plays them back three months later.
  • Hire people who really care and let them argue. People need to feel ownership.
  • A CEO in the details: Mark rearranges the rest of the company (infrastructure, data centres) around MSL. Global optimization instead of local inertia.

John’s “4 things most CEOs wouldn’t do”

  1. Publicly calling the previous effort not good enough, and rebooting it.
  2. Paying “ungodly amounts” for talent, including 49% of Scale.
  3. Hiring a lab head who had never trained a frontier model.
  4. Clearing the CEO’s calendar for two months to recruit.

The anecdote that showed a reboot was needed

In one of Alex’s first meetings, a leader from the existing effort argued that consumers didn’t need a reasoning model. Alex’s reaction was “Oh, no, I’ve joined the wrong company”, and he called Nat right after. The lesson he drew was that leadership needed to be far more “AI-pilled”. Once Mark was, the rest of the leadership followed. Alex cites Adam Mosseri’s personal setup of vibe-coded mini-apps. Separately, Mark mentions bringing in Kunal Shah (founder of CRED) to run WhatsApp and push commerce in India.
